# Stage — the combined vision

**What Stage is.**
Stage is a place where you don't have to be a big business to have a home online — and where a customer feels like they discovered a person, not just a product. It's built on a theatre metaphor because that's not decoration, it's the actual mechanic: performers put something of themselves on stage, and the audience comes not just to buy, but to watch, understand, and root for someone.

**The feeling, made real without a camera yet.**
The original idea for Stage was built on video — short, raw, emotional clips where a seller's feelings and inspiration were as much the product as the product itself. That instinct hasn't been dropped, it's been translated. When a performer creates their Stage, they're asked one honest question early in setup: why did you start this, or what do you want people to feel when they see your work. That answer sits right under their business name on the storefront, above the products, so a customer sees the person before they see the price. When a performer posts an update — a restock, a new drop — there's room for why, not just what. "Back in stock" is information. "Back in stock, this sold out in two days and I've been waiting to bring it back" is the feeling the original vision was chasing. No video required to start. The moment sellers are ready to be on camera, this is exactly the seed that grows into it.

**How someone becomes a performer.**
A performer builds their Stage before they ever register an account. They open the app, choose "I'm here to perform," and start filling in what their Stage looks like — business name, logo, description, their opening note, products with photos and prices, and the categories they want to sell under, which they name themselves. Nothing is locked into Stage's own category tree; a perfume seller might have "Arabian Mists" and "Gift Sets," a clothing seller might have "Two Pieces" and "Baby Tops." Stage doesn't presume to know their business better than they do. Once it's built, they preview it exactly as a customer would see it — "Looks good? Let's put it on Stage" — and only then do they register their business details to save and launch it. When it's live: "Your Stage is ready."

From there, a performer's world is deliberately small and calm. **My Stage** is where they manage the storefront, products, categories, and updates. **Backstage** is order management — see what's come in, accept it, decline it, move it along. **Stage Buzz** gives them a light read on how they're doing — views, shares, followers, nothing more elaborate than that yet. The goal is a performer who can run their whole presence without needing to stay glued to a phone answering every message themselves.

**How someone becomes the audience.**
Someone opens the app and chooses "I'm here for the show." They're asked what they're into — fashion, food, beauty, gifts, tech, decor, whatever draws them — and can skip this entirely if they want. That's not a permanent box; it's just a starting point for what they see first. From there, their world has four rooms: **Your Stage**, a feed shaped by what they picked; **Explore**, for searching and discovering beyond that, including the shout bubbles that let a business's presence be felt before it's been searched for; **Following**, where the sellers they've chosen to keep up with live; and **Orders**, tracking what they've bought and where it stands.

**Discovering a business, not a product.**
This is the part that separates Stage from an ordinary marketplace. On Explore, what a customer meets first is a business — its name, its category, a glimpse of what it makes, and a way in. Tapping that business takes them into a real storefront: the business's identity and their opening note at the top, a Follow and Share option right there, then their self-named categories, then the products. Only after all of that does a customer land on a single product page — full images, price, description, whether it's in stock, and, if it applies, how they can make it theirs.

**Making it theirs.**
Not every product is fixed. Some are configurable — pick a size, pick a scent, pick from a small set of options the seller already defined. Some are personalized — a name to engrave, a message, a font, a colour, filled in by the customer and carried with the order. And some are open — "tell us what you'd like" — where the seller reviews what's asked for and confirms it themselves before anything is made. This isn't a sprawling customization engine. It's three honest tiers: fixed, choose from options, or tell us what you want.

**Buying, without pretending it's simple.**
When someone adds things to their cart from different sellers, Stage doesn't flatten that into one fake order. The cart shows each seller's items and subtotal separately, with an overall total sitting on top so the customer still sees what they're about to spend in one glance. At checkout, each seller's portion becomes its own order, moving through its own simple lifecycle — pending, confirmed, processing, ready, completed — visible to the performer in Backstage and to the customer under Orders.

**Drops — a scheduled reveal.**
Not every product has to just appear in a feed. A performer can build toward a moment — a small batch of new pieces, a restock, a limited run — and let anticipation do some of the work a video once did. On the day, the audience sees a curtain, not the products yet: a stage set, a countdown, a chance to peek behind the curtain for a blurred hint of what's coming. When the moment arrives, the curtain opens and the pieces are revealed together, all at once, the way a performance actually starts. Someone who doesn't want to wait can set a reminder and come back right on time. This isn't a separate feature bolted onto Stage — it's the theatre metaphor doing what theatre does: building toward a moment, together, instead of dropping items into a feed one by one.

**What Stage isn't doing yet, on purpose.**
Stage isn't handling payment directly yet — that stays between customer and seller for now, while Stage focuses on the parts it can build well: discovery, the storefront, the cart, the order trail. There's no AI recommendation engine, no sales chatbot, no haggling, no coupons, no in-app budget limiter, no delivery network, no advertising, and no commission structure locked in yet. None of that is abandoned — it's set aside on purpose, because the question for right now is simpler: does this help someone discover, explore, connect with, or buy from a seller. If not, it waits.

**The throughline.**
Strip away everything Stage doesn't have yet, and what's left is the exact same sentence the very first version of this idea was written around: a place where you can shine, so let's put on a show. The show just starts with words instead of video, with a storefront instead of a skit, and with a slow, buildable version of trust instead of an algorithm that needs data it doesn't have yet. Nothing about the soul of it changed. Only the size of the first step did.

### A note on what is, and isn't, in this version

- **Shout bubbles** — included. They're named directly in the Explore section as part of how a business's presence can be felt before it's searched for.
- **Drops** — included. Folded in as its own part of the vision, tied to the theatre metaphor rather than treated as a bolted-on feature.
- **Video feed** — not included as a built feature. The document is explicit that video isn't required to start; the performer's short written note is the current stand-in for the feeling video was meant to carry, and it's built as a seed the video feature can grow from later.
